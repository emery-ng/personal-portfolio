AI & Human Behavior

What happens to us when AI does the thinking?

Research · USF Behavioral AI Lab · In progress

Ask a college student to do something that isn't hard, only slow, and ask what they reach for first. In my experience, more than half the time, the answer is AI. I understand why. I've seen how much it helps: cleaning a big, messy dataset used to take hours, and with AI it takes thirty minutes or less. For students, it's a tutor that steps in the moment they're stuck. AI has spread faster than almost any technology before it, and it's easy to see why we keep handing it more of our work.

But when we hand off the effort, we hand off the practice that comes with it. That's cognitive offloading: whenever a task feels like work, AI is now the easiest way around it, and the more we take that route, the more we depend on it. The dependence doesn't stay with tasks. It reaches our decisions: what to buy, what to eat, what to cook. And if one day we can't make a decision for ourselves, what is the point of being human?

That question is behind the research I'm part of at USF's Behavioral AI Lab, led by Dr. Triparna de Vreede, who studies the quiet effects AI has on how people behave, think, and feel, the changes we don't notice while they happen. Our version of the question is a small one: what happens to boredom? When every idle moment can be filled, we may lose the ability to sit with a dull one. And dull moments matter, because that's often when an idea arrives, as we step away from a problem and our minds keep working on it in the background. We hypothesize that heavy AI use quietly erodes that capacity, and with it, creativity. The literature review and the model are complete, and we're designing the study to test it.

If the evidence supports it, we want to take the next step: understanding which parts of how AI tools are built shape these effects, so the people who design them, and the people who write the rules for them, have evidence to work with. The goal isn't to argue against AI. It's to use it with our eyes open, and to catch what it's changing in us before it's too late.
